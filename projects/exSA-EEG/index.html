Time-course of brain processing of emotional communication

Overview

This study investigated how quickly the brain recognizes the communicative function of emotion-related utterances. Using EEG, we compared brain responses to expressive sentences such as “I am happy”, which communicate the speaker’s emotional state, with similar assertive sentences such as “He is happy”, which report information about someone else.

Can we detect communicative intent (expressive vs. assertive) directly from electrophysiological brain signals (EEG), and how early does this differentiation occur in the language comprehension process?

Further resources:

  • Publication: Boux, I. P., Osterloh, M. R., Tomasello, R., Grisoni, L., & Pulvermüller, F. (2026). Instantaneous emotion sharing: early brain signatures of understanding expressive communicative actions. Neuropsychologia, 109395. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2026.109395

Data collection

  • Participants: 24 subjects (after exclusions)
  • Signals: 64-channel electroencephalography (EEG)
  • Sampling rate: 500 Hz
[Figure 1]: Illustration of the EEG data collection system. (Image generated by ChatGPT by OpenAI.)

While EEG is being recorded, participants are seated in front of a computer and take part in a passive computerized task in which they read the presented text. The text consists of more than 270 sentences that result from crossing two independent factors, grammatical person (1st and 3rd) and adjective type (emotional vs. physical), in a 2 x 2 experimental design. This effectively results in four types of sentences, for instance:

  • 1st/emotional: “I am happy.” (expressive)
  • 3rd/emotional: “He is happy.” (assertive)
  • 1st/physical: “I am blonde.” (assertive)
  • 3rd/physical: “He is blonde.” (assertive)

Importantly, expressive communication is instantiated only in the “1st/emotional” case, as confirmed by validation ratings from an independent sample of participants.

Preprocessing Pipeline

The EEG data from the 64 channels is preprocessed according to standard event-related-potential practice. The aim is to go from a continuous signal recorded across 64 channels to averaged brain responses to the critical word (happy or blonde).

Results

EEG data is multidimensional: it extends in time (ms elapsed since the word of interest appeared on screen) and space (64 channels measuring signals across the head). The first step was to narrow down the time dimension and identify time windows of interest. For this purpose, the EEG signal across all channels and subjects was averaged into a single signal. The peaks and troughs in this signal were identified using the full width at half maximum approach (FWHM). This resulted in the identification of four time windows of interest (see Figure 2):

  • TW1: 76–100 ms
  • TW2: 110–130 ms
  • TW3: 143–197 ms
  • TW4: 221–279 ms
[Figure 2]: Figure and legend taken from Boux et al. (2026). Grey boxes indicate the four unbiased data-driven time windows defined around each peak using the FWHM method. The yellow boxes indicate the three a priori time windows taken from the literature, capturing the N400, early P600 (eP600) and late P600 (lP600). The upper banner shows the average topographical maps obtained for each of these time windows.

The second step was to simplify the spatial dimension of the EEG data. To do so, we split the spatial component into two dimensions, or factors:

  • gradient: anterior, central, posterior
  • laterality: left, midline, right

This resulted in nine selections of electrodes (see Figure 2):

  • anterior/left
  • anterior/midline
  • anterior/right
  • central/left
  • central/midline
  • central/right
  • posterior/left
  • posterior/midline
  • posterior/right

After identifying channel poolings and time windows of interest, we aimed to assess in which of these time windows and electrode subsets we would detect a dissociation between expressives and assertives. This dissociation was assessed using a repeated-measures ANOVA with the following factors, carried out separately in the four time windows of interest:

  • 2 x Person: 1st vs 3rd
  • 2 x Adjective: emotional vs physical
  • 3 x Gradient: anterior, central, posterior
  • 3 x Laterality: left, midline, right

The expected key interaction between Person and Adjective was detected in TW1 (76–100 ms) and TW4 (221–279 ms). The effect in TW1 was not robust across preprocessing approaches, so we consider it unreliable. The effect in TW4, however, was robust across preprocessing approaches and was detected at central and posterior electrodes. This interaction indicated a more-than-additive combination of brain signals as a function of Person and Adjective, characteristic of the expressive speech act. In other words, the brain differentiates communicative function surprisingly early in the processing stream, and possibly in parallel with other linguistic aspects of the comprehended sentence.

Conclusion

The results showed very early neural differences between expressive and assertive communicative actions, suggesting that pragmatic meaning, that is the communicative intent of language, is processed rapidly and in parallel with other aspects of language comprehension.
